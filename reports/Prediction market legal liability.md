# Calling it a marketplace won't protect you

> **This is not legal advice.** This report synthesizes research notes for a non-lawyer founder as of 13 November 2026. It is a map of the risks, not an opinion you can rely on. **Before your platform touches real money, or anything convertible to money (deposits, withdrawals, prizes, redeemable coins, crypto), hire a lawyer who specializes in US gaming law and CFTC/derivatives regulation, and do not launch until they sign off.** Several of the crimes described below can be charged against you personally, and some carry multi-year prison terms.

A US founder who runs a real-money, peer-to-peer yes/no exchange with user-created markets, escrowed stakes and a 10% cut of winners' profit would face stacked federal and state liability. **Structuring it as "mostly a marketplace" would remove none of it.** Under the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA), the violation is *operating* the venue, not taking positions. The CFTC charged both Intrade and Polymarket, which were venues where customers traded against each other, for running unregistered markets ([CFTC Release 6423-12](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/6423-12); [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/cftc-issues-cease-and-desist-order-to-binary-options-operator-using-smart-contracts)). Under state gambling codes built on the New York model, someone who "materially aids" gambling or "participates in the proceeds" is by definition not a mere player, and those offenses target exactly such people ([NY Penal Law § 225.00](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._penal_law_section_225.00)). Your three defining features make things worse. The escrow adds money-transmission and "stakeholder" exposure, the 10% commission is the textbook house "vig", and having creators resolve their own markets leaves you as the adjudicator who releases the funds. The federal Illegal Gambling Business Act reaches whoever "conducts, finances, manages, supervises, directs, or owns" such a business, so a company structure does not shield the founder ([18 U.S.C. 1955](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1955)). Even CFTC-registered Kalshi has faced criminal charges in Arizona, a New York Attorney General suit claiming $36B, and more than a dozen consumer class actions in 2025–2026. An unregistered site would have none of Kalshi's federal-preemption defenses. The good news is that **your current play-money demo, with no deposits, withdrawals or prizes, sits outside the core of all of this**, and your recent design changes are sound. **The clear recommendation is to stay play-money**, monetize in ways that never give users anything of value, and reach real money only by plugging into an existing CFTC-registered exchange or, for a true exchange, by obtaining a UK licence.

**How solid the evidence is.** All five underlying research notes report that direct fetches of primary sources were blocked during research. These included cftc.gov, federalregister.gov, uscode.house.gov, ecfr.gov, state legislature sites and the UK Gambling Commission. As a result, **most 2025–2026 developments rest on search-engine snippets of news, law-firm alerts and trackers, not on verified primary text.** Statutory wording was stated from established knowledge and not re-fetched. Penalty maxima and thresholds are marked "not re-verified". Cost figures from consultant or vendor blogs are flagged in the cost table. Throughout this report, **"(snippet-based)"** marks findings where trackers conflict or only secondary summaries were available. Your lawyer should verify these first.

## Federal law treats the venue itself as the violation

### Commodities law: an unregistered event-contract exchange is illegal however neutral it is

The CFTC has long treated a cash-settled yes/no contract that pays $1 as a "swap" or commodity option. The statutory swap definition covers any agreement providing for a payment "dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence" ([7 U.S.C. 1a](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title7-section1a&num=0&edition=prelim), text not re-fetched). Two provisions follow from that. First, **retail users who are not "eligible contract participants" may trade swaps only on a CFTC-designated contract market (DCM)**. That status generally requires more than $10M invested, so ordinary website users do not qualify. Second, **no one may operate a facility for trading swaps without registering as a DCM or a swap execution facility** ([7 U.S.C. 2](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title7-section2&num=0&edition=prelim); [7 U.S.C. 7b-3](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title7-section7b-3&num=0&edition=prelim)). On 9 October 2026 the CFTC proposed writing "event contracts, including those based on sports-, politics-, cultural, and weather-related events" expressly into the swap definition. The proposal was published in the Federal Register on 19 October ([CFTC Release 9310-26](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9310-26); [Federal Register 2026-21377](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/19/2026-21377/further-definition-of-swap-inclusions)). The comment deadline was reported as 18 November 2026 by a single source (snippet-based).

The enforcement template is well worn. In 2012 the CFTC sued Ireland-based Intrade for letting US customers trade off-exchange binary options, saying it is unlawful to solicit US persons "even if they are called 'prediction' contracts" ([CFTC 6423-12](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/6423-12)). Intrade lost on summary judgment and was ordered to pay **$3M** ([CFTC 7758-18](https://cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/7758-18)). In January 2022 Polymarket, a peer-to-peer venue that had hosted more than 900 markets, settled for **$1.4M**, a figure reduced for cooperation. It also had to wind down its markets and refund users for failing to register as a DCM or swap execution facility ([Baker McKenzie](https://blockchain.bakermckenzie.com/2022/01/05/us-cftc-issues-1-4-million-fine-against-crypto-betting-service-polymarket-based-on-its-operating-swaps-without-proper-registration)). Holding both sides' stakes adds a further problem. An entity that accepts orders for swaps and takes money to secure them fits the definition of a futures commission merchant, a separately registered role with strict rules on segregating customer funds ([7 U.S.C. 1a](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title7-section1a&num=0&edition=prelim); [7 U.S.C. 6d](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title7-section6d&num=0&edition=prelim)). When Polymarket came back to the US in 2025, it did not argue it was "just a marketplace". It **bought a licensed exchange and clearinghouse (QCEX) for about $112M** ([Blockworks](https://blockworks.co/news/polymarket-qcex-acquisition)).

**Penalties reach you personally.** Under the CEA, anyone who aids or abets a violation is liable as a principal. So is anyone who "controls" the violator, unless they acted in good faith and did not induce the violation. Federal courts can impose per-violation penalties of the greater of a statutory amount or **triple the monetary gain**, and willful violations are felonies carrying up to 10 years ([7 U.S.C. 13c](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/13c); [7 U.S.C. 13a-1](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title7-section13a-1&num=0&edition=prelim); [7 U.S.C. 13](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title7-section13&num=0&edition=prelim); maxima not re-verified). Willfulness has an uncomfortable implication: **a founder who goes live after reading an analysis like this one is in a worse position than Polymarket's founders were in 2020.**

The current CFTC under Chairman Michael Selig is friendly to prediction markets. It withdrew the 2024 proposal to ban political and sports contracts on 4 February 2026 ([Sidley](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2026/02/us-cftc-signals-imminent-rulemaking-on-prediction-markets)). In June 2026 it proposed case-by-case "public interest" review ([Federal Register 2026-11854](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/12/2026-11854/prediction-markets-public-interest-determinations)). But all of that friendliness runs through registration, and none of it creates a path for unregistered venues. The 2025 closure of the DOJ and CFTC probes into Polymarket ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/regulation/2025-07-15-us-justice-department-and-cftc-close-probes-into-polymarket-bloomberg-362703)) legalized nothing. A five-year limitations period means a future, less friendly Commission can still act on what you do today. A staff "no-action letter" like PredictIt's is not a realistic route either. PredictIt's 2014 letter rested on non-profit academic status and caps on traders and stakes, it was withdrawn in 2022, and PredictIt survived only through litigation ([Covers](https://www.covers.com/industry/predictit-and-cftc-reach-settlement-in-longstanding-lawsuit)).

### Federal gambling statutes: low thresholds and personal reach

The **Illegal Gambling Business Act ([18 U.S.C. § 1955](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title18-section1955&num=0&edition=prelim)) is the most dangerous federal statute for a small startup.** It applies when four conditions are met. The business violates the law of a state where it operates, which most states' bans on unlicensed online wagering supply. It involves five or more people who conduct, manage, finance or own it, a count reached by two founders, a developer, a support contractor and a moderator. And it runs for more than 30 days or takes in $2,000 in gross revenue in a single day. The penalty is up to 5 years in prison plus forfeiture of "any property, including money, used in violation", which includes customer escrow ([CRS 97-619](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20120124_97-619_93d3541d977fd6a8ee3f734360512ef731ed8be7.html); thresholds not re-verified).

The **Wire Act** (18 U.S.C. § 1084, up to 2 years) covers people "engaged in the business of betting or wagering" who transmit bets over the internet. The First Circuit held in 2021 that it covers only sporting events or contests ([NH Lottery Comm'n v. Rosen](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca1/19-1835/19-1835-2021-01-20.html)). That makes your sports markets squarely exposed and your political, economic and culture markets probably outside its reach. The Act does not require the defendant to take the risk the way a bookmaker does. Running the book, holding stakes and taking a volume-linked cut fits "engaged in the business". No US court has yet ruled on a pure peer-to-peer exchange under the Act.

**UIGEA** makes it a crime, punishable by up to 5 years, for a gambling business to accept card, ACH or wire payments connected with internet gambling that is unlawful under state or federal law. The decisive detail is its **express exclusion for transactions "on or subject to the rules of a registered entity" under the CEA**. That exclusion protects Kalshi and Polymarket US and offers nothing to an unregistered site ([31 U.S.C. § 5362](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title31-section5362&num=0&edition=prelim); not re-verified). Its implementing rule, Regulation GG, requires banks and card networks to block such payments, so in practice mainstream processors will refuse or drop you ([Fed. Reg. 2008 final rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2008-11-18/html/E8-27181.htm)). Prosecutors routinely add Travel Act, RICO and money-laundering counts. The 2011 "Black Friday" poker case ended in a **$731M** forfeiture settlement and guilty pleas by individuals ([DOJ SDNY](https://www.justice.gov/archive/usao/nys/pressreleases/July12/pokersettlement.html)). In February 2026, the Manhattan US Attorney's Office (SDNY) said that calling a venue a "prediction market" does not insulate it from liability ([Duane Morris, snippet-based](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/southern_district_new_york_us_attorneys_office_examines_laws_prediction_markets_0226.html)).

| Statute | Reaches the founder? | Maximum prison (not re-verified) |
|---|---|---|
| [18 U.S.C. § 1955](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title18-section1955&num=0&edition=prelim) (illegal gambling business) | Yes: "conducts, finances, manages, supervises, directs, or owns" | 5 years + forfeiture |

### Money transmission, securities and tax add independent problems

**Escrow is money transmission.** Accepting Alice's and Bob's stakes and later paying the winner fits FinCEN's definition of a money transmitter: "the acceptance of currency, funds, or other value … from one person and the transmission of … funds … to another location or person" ([31 CFR 1010.100](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1010/subpart-A/section-1010.100); not re-verified). The payment-processor exemption does not fit this model. The "integral to the provision of services" exemption is your best argument, but using it concedes that the underlying service is gambling. Operating without FinCEN registration and state licences is an independent felony under § 1960. An unlicensed operator is not a regulated "casino" under the Bank Secrecy Act, because that status requires a state licence ([FinCEN Casino FAQs](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Casino_FAQs_Final.pdf)). Stripe lists internet gambling among prohibited businesses (snippet-based, [Stripe](https://stripe.com/us/restricted-businesses/)). The research could not verify the cost and timeline of state money-transmitter licensing.

**Securities law** matters only for some markets, but sharply. A plain binary contract between users is generally not an "investment contract" under *Howey*. A user-created market such as "Will Apple beat EPS consensus?" or "Will Tesla close above $300?" is a **security-based swap**. Those may not be offered to retail investors off a registered national securities exchange ([15 U.S.C. § 78c(a)(68)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title15-section78c&num=0&edition=prelim)). The SEC has been weighing its event-contract jurisdiction throughout 2026 and paused about 24 prediction-market ETFs in May ([CPA Practice Advisor](https://cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/05/21/sec-chief-atkins-pumps-the-brakes-on-prediction-market-funds/183777)).

**Tax** would be unpleasant even for an illegal business, because illegal income is taxable. The federal wagering excise tax is **2% of the amount wagered** (not of your fee) for wagers not authorized by state law. It applies to anyone running a "wagering pool … conducted for profit" on sports, and an annual occupational tax applies on top ([26 U.S.C. § 4401](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4401); [§ 4421](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4421)). On a sports market priced at 90¢, your 10% fee on the winner's profit is about 1¢ per $1 staked, while the excise tax would be 2¢. Whether an exchange that never books bets owes the tax at all is unsettled. Your users face a separate change. From 2026, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) caps gambling-loss deductions at **90%**, so a user who wins and loses $100,000 owes tax on $10,000 of "phantom" income ([CasinoBeats](https://casinobeats.com/2025/07/04/big-beautiful-bill-gambling-deduction-cap-2026/)). The IRS has issued no guidance on how prediction-market winnings should be reported.

## States are winning even against federally registered exchanges

State law is where the "marketplace" defence collapses most clearly. New York's definitions, copied in many states, cover staking value on "a contest of chance **or a future contingent event** not under [your] control or influence". That second prong takes in elections, economic data and sports, so forecasting skill is no defence ([NY Senate PEN 225.00](https://nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PEN/225.00)). Bettors who merely stake and collect are "players". Anyone who "materially aids" gambling, including through "acceptance of money risked", or who "participates in the proceeds" commits promoting-gambling offences ([newyork.public.law](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._penal_law_section_225.00)). **Your escrow is "acceptance of money risked", and your 10% is "participating in the proceeds."** Other states go further. In Washington, knowingly transmitting or receiving gambling information over the internet is a **class C felony** ([RCW 9.46.240](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=9.46.240)). Nevada's statute reaches anyone accepting wagers from Nevadans "within or outside of this state" ([NRS ch. 465](https://www.leg.state.nv.us/NRS/NRS-465.html)). In October 2025 the Illinois Gaming Board declared that "'Prediction markets' constitute gambling activity under Illinois law" ([Springfield Business Journal](https://www.springfieldbusinessjournal.com/news/illinois-regulators-say-prediction-markets-areillegal-gambling-but-bettors-love-them/)). The research could not verify the current text of the California, Texas and Illinois statutes or the exact penalty grades in New York.

The 2025–2026 enforcement wave targeted exchanges that *are* CFTC-registered, and the states mostly won. **Arizona** filed 20 criminal misdemeanour counts against Kalshi on 17 March 2026, four of them over election contracts ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/17/arizona-kalshi-criminal-misdemeanor-charges.html)). The **New York Attorney General** sued Kalshi on 31 July 2026, seeking treble damages and $100,000 per solicitation, a total the AG calculated at **$36B** ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/410247/new-york-sues-kalshi)). **Massachusetts** won a preliminary injunction, which is stayed pending appeal ([Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-secures-court-order-that-will-block-kalshi-from-offering-unlawful-sports-wagers-in-massachusetts)), and **Nevada's** regulator obtained a temporary restraining order against Polymarket ([SBC Americas](https://sbcamericas.com/2026/02/02/nevada-judge-initial-ban-polymarket/)).

On appeal, the **Ninth Circuit (28 August 2026)** and **Sixth Circuit (25 September 2026)** held that Kalshi's sports contracts are likely not swaps. The Sixth Circuit added that even if they were, state gambling law is not preempted ([FindLaw](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/us-9th-circuit/359613.html); [Sportico](https://www.sportico.com/law/analysis/2026/kalshi-circuit-split-scotus-1234945840/)). Only the **Third Circuit (6 April 2026, 2–1)** sided with Kalshi, and it did so expressly because the contracts trade "on CFTC-licensed exchanges" ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/federal-appeals-court-cftc-jurisdiction-over-sports-event-contracts)). Supreme Court petitions are pending, with no grant found as of mid-November. Kalshi's response was reportedly due 7 December 2026 (snippet-based). Trackers conflict on whether the Ninth Circuit denied rehearing en banc. The Fourth Circuit's Maryland decision was still pending as of a 10 November tracker (snippet-based). Tribes have opened a further front: on 4 November 2026 a federal court barred Kalshi's sports contracts on two California tribes' lands under the federal Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA) ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/business/2026-11-06-kalshi-injunction-california-tribal-lands-435813)). **The lesson for you is simple: Kalshi's entire defence is federal preemption through CFTC registration. An unregistered site has no preemption argument at all, and states would treat it like an offshore sportsbook.**

Civil exposure scales with your users' losses, not with your revenue. Old "loss recovery" statutes let losers sue to recover gambling losses, and in some states let anyone sue for treble damages afterwards. Kentucky used its statute to win an approximately **$870M** judgment against PokerStars' owner. That judgment was reversed and then reinstated in 2020, and a later report describes the final recovery as about $300M; the two figures could not be reconciled ([Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/kentucky-court-upends-870m-pokerstars-judgment/); [Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/southeast/2021/01/20/597975.htm)). New York's version lets losers sue the "stakeholder", and an escrow-holding platform is the natural target (statute text not verified). Bloomberg Law counts **more than a dozen consumer class actions** against Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and DraftKings under state consumer-protection laws ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/prediction-markets-face-new-threat-in-consumer-protection-suits)).

State licensing offers no route for your product. New Jersey licensed the first US peer-to-peer sports exchanges in 2022, but Prophet Exchange left in 2024, and liquidity is trapped within each state ([Legal Sports Report](https://www.legalsportsreport.com/183374/prophet-exchange-offline-in-nj-sports-betting-market/)). **The research found no state that licenses a user-created, any-topic event exchange.** State licences cover sports only, and most states exclude elections entirely. Reports that Illinois enacted a prediction-market licensing bill (SB 3019) in June 2026 conflict with reports of a prohibition bill and remain unverified.

## Platform-design risks follow the money, not the label

**Founders' personal liability.** A company protects you against contract claims and ordinary debts. It does not protect you against statutes that name the person who runs or owns the business, against CEA controlling-person liability, or against Bank Secrecy Act charges. BitMEX's three co-founders pleaded guilty personally in 2022 for *failing to maintain KYC/AML programmes*. They were not accused of stealing anything, and they were pardoned in 2025 ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-03-28/trump-pardons-bitmex-co-founders-arthur-hayes-benjamin-delo-and-samuel-reed)). The FBI searched Polymarket's CEO's home in November 2024 ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/justice-department-ends-investigation-polymarket)). Both stories ended well for the founders only because of politics, and politics change. Insurance will not save you either. D&O policies for crypto and gambling-adjacent firms often carry regulatory exclusions, and an executive at one specialist insurer said a regulatory exclusion "erodes the value of the D&O policy almost entirely" ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/in-house-counsel/crypto-risks-prompt-uptick-in-insurance-exclusions)).

**Section 230 does almost nothing for a real-money exchange.** It expressly does not impair federal criminal enforcement ([47 U.S.C. 230](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230)). Courts treat a platform's own transactions as outside its protection. In *HomeAway v. Santa Monica* the Ninth Circuit held that completing bookings is "distinct, internal, and nonpublic" conduct, not publishing ([Eric Goldman](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2019/05/ninth-circuit-chunks-another-section-230-ruling-homeaway-v-santa-monica-catch-up-post.htm)). A federal court refused Section 230 protection to Apple, Google and Meta for processing virtual-chip payments in third-party casino apps ([Eric Goldman](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2022/09/section-230-doesnt-protect-app-stores-that-sell-virtual-chips-for-casino-apps-in-re-apple-app-store.htm)). That ruling is on appeal in 2026. You would be doing far more than processing payments: matching, escrowing, settling and taking a cut. Section 230 might help against a defamation claim over a user-written market title. Ironically, that protection is strongest in **your current play-money product**, where no transactions of value exist.

**Harmful markets.** Permissionless creation produces death and assassination markets within days. On Augur in 2018, markets on whether Trump, Buffett, McCain or Bezos would die or be killed appeared almost at launch ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2018/07/26/2449/the-latest-blockchain-use-case-anonymously-betting-on-public-figure-death-pools/)). Even large players remove such markets only after backlash. Polymarket pulled a nuclear-detonation market in March 2026 ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/ecosystems/2026-03-04-polymarket-removes-nuclear-detonation-market-392161)) and apologized for a market on downed US pilots in April ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/04/06/polymarket-apology-market-downed-f-15-us-pilot-insider-trading-iran-war/)). The CFTC's June 2026 proposal reads "terrorism, assassination and war" broadly. A contract on a foreign leader leaving office could be caught unless it expressly excludes those outcomes ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20260618-10-takeaways-from-the-cftc-event-contracts-proposed-rulemaking)). Your banned-topic list and user reports are the right instinct. **Pre-listing review would be stronger than after-the-fact reports** once anything of value is at stake.

**Insider trading and manipulation.** In 2026 the CFTC brought its first insider-trading cases involving event contracts. In *CFTC v. Van Dyke* (April), a service member who helped plan the Maduro capture allegedly made more than $404K trading Polymarket contracts on it, and a parallel criminal indictment followed ([Baker Botts](https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2026/april/cftc-brings-first-of-its-kind-insider-trading-action)). In August, a former White House teleprompter operator was ordered to pay about $172,000, and the CFTC credited Kalshi's surveillance for catching it ([The Block](https://theblock.co/news/regulation/2026-08-29-former-white-house-teleprompter-operator-ordered-to-pay-172000-for-kalshi-trades-on-mention-markets-413058)). User-created markets on small events, such as a YouTuber's next video or a local official's vote, are where one participant can decide the outcome. Your rule barring creators from trading their own markets addresses the most obvious case. With real money it would also need KYC, so that a creator's second account can be detected, plus surveillance and the contractual right to void trades.

**Resolution disputes.** Polymarket shows what can go wrong when resolution is delegated. In March 2025 a token-holder "whale" with about 25% of the UMA oracle's votes resolved a roughly $7M market as YES although no deal had been signed, and Polymarket refused refunds ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/ecosystems/2025-03-26-polymarket-says-governance-attack-by-uma-whale-to-hijack-a-bets-resolution-is-unprecedented-348171)). Kalshi shows that even a written rule can fail. Its approximately $54M Khamenei market settled under a death carve-out, and a class action followed alleging the carve-out was not prominently disclosed (*Risch v. KalshiEX*, C.D. Cal., filed 5 March 2026). Kalshi reimbursed fees and net losses ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bloomberg-government-news/kalshi-sued-over-death-carveout-in-iran-leader-prediction-market)). **Creator resolution with a 24-hour dispute window is a reasonable play-money mechanism. With real money it would leave you as escrow agent and final adjudicator**, exposed to breach-of-contract, good-faith, conversion and consumer-protection claims that Section 230 does not cover. The protections that matter are criteria and named sources fixed before trading, carve-outs shown at the point of trade, and void-and-refund rules for ambiguity.

## Lawful paths, ranked by cost and liability

The research converges on one ordering. Play money carries the least liability. Next comes acting as an introducing broker or front end for a registered exchange, then a UK licence, then your own DCM. State exchange licences, offshore operation and sweepstakes carry the most. Figures marked *estimate* are inferences in the research notes, not reported numbers.

Two structural facts shape these choices. **Even a DCM cannot host your exact product.** Each contract must be self-certified by the exchange, and the CFTC's July 2026 Advisory 26-22 discourages broad "template" certifications ([CFTC Release 9273-26](https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases/9273-26)). The feasible regulated version is "user proposes, exchange reviews and lists". **The partner route is how the big players entered:** Robinhood, Webull and Coinbase distribute Kalshi contracts, and DraftKings and Fanatics reportedly use an introducing broker with a third-party futures commission merchant ([DeFi Rate](https://defirate.com/news/prediction-market-land-grab-dcms-fcms-and-the-race-to-control-the-stack/)). No evidence was found that any DCM lets a third-party front end create new markets. Finding a willing partner is the binding constraint.

The **play-money conclusion** deserves its own caveat. No CFTC statement or court decision squarely holds that play-money markets fall outside the CEA or state gambling law. The conclusion rests on statutory logic: with no payment there is no swap, and with no consideration or prize there is no gambling. It also rests on the enforcement record, since every action found involved real money or crypto. Manifold, the leading play-money market, ran a redeemable "sweepcash" layer from about September 2024 and shut it down in March 2025, citing a regulatory landscape that had "changed significantly" ([Manifold investor update](https://manifoldmarkets.notion.site/Investor-update-3-6-25-1ac54492ea7a8022aaf1ed2cd188663d)). Manifold sells play currency for real money while offering no cash-out ([FantasyLabs](https://www.fantasylabs.com/?p=190629)). Risk returns if any purchased or earned balance can be redeemed for value, if leaderboards pay prizes, if accounts or tokens can be traded on secondary markets, or if per-user charity redemption is offered, which is a gray area.

### UK and EU comparison

The **UK is the most natural home for your exact model.** Betfair, Smarkets and Matchbook operate peer-to-peer exchanges that charge commission on winnings under a Gambling Commission **Remote Betting Intermediary** operating licence ([GC](https://www.gamblingcommission.gov.uk/licensees-and-businesses/licences-and-fees/remote-betting-intermediary-operating-licence)). The regulatory fees are modest. The application fee rises from £185 to £231 on 1 October 2026, and an outdated 2017 schedule set the lowest annual remote-intermediary fee at £5,282 ([GC](https://www.gamblingcommission.gov.uk/licensees-and-businesses/guide/page/how-much-youll-pay); [SI 2017/303](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2017/303/data.html)). The real costs are compliance and the UK entity itself: personal management licences, AML and affordability checks, segregation of customer funds, technical standards and remote gaming duty (snippet-based; the Gambling Commission's site was not fetchable). Markets on financial prices may fall under FCA rules on contracts for difference and spread bets rather than gambling law. **The EU has no passport.** Gambling is licensed country by country. About 30 regulators have acted against Polymarket since 2024, and France ordered ISPs to block it on 16 July 2026 ([ANJ](https://anj.fr/promotion-illegal-gambling-services-blocking-polymarket-website)). A UK licence does not authorize serving US users, and a US founder still controls the business, so this route works only as a genuinely non-US operation set up with UK and US counsel.

## Conclusion

The "marketplace" theory fails for a structural reason, not a technical one. Every regime that matters here, from commodities and gambling law to money transmission and loss-recovery statutes, regulates the person who **runs the venue, holds the money and takes a cut**. Your design does all three. Neutrality between bettors is exactly what the CFTC regulates as an exchange and what New York-style codes call "advancing gambling". The escrow and the 10% fee are the evidence of it. The 2026 landscape adds a twist that is easy to miss. The friendlier CFTC and the Kalshi litigation have not opened space for small operators. They have created a two-tier world in which registered exchanges fight states with a preemption argument and unregistered ones have no argument at all, while states, tribes, attorneys general and class-action lawyers are all better organized than they were two years ago.

**Recommendation:** keep the product play-money and treat that as a deliberate legal moat, not a stopgap. Never let a balance become redeemable, never pay leaderboard prizes, and keep your new safeguards: the creator trading ban, the dispute window, the banned topics and user reports. Add pre-listing review for sensitive topics. If real money becomes the goal, the realistic route is to distribute a registered exchange's contracts as a guaranteed introducing broker, accepting that you will lose your own order book, instant user-created markets and the 10%-of-winnings fee. The only route that preserves the true exchange model is a non-US operation under a UK licence. Either step requires a gaming and CFTC lawyer engaged *before* any money moves. Knowingly launching a real-money US version now would turn an ordinary regulatory violation into a willful one.
