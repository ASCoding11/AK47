## 1. Does an operator who takes a commission on bets between other people commit state gambling offenses? (NY, CA, WA, TX, NV, IL; player-vs-operator)

### Takeaway
Yes. US state gambling codes, most built on the Model Penal Code / New York template, aim their serious offenses at whoever *advances* or *profits from* gambling other than as a mere "player". Taking a cut of winnings is the textbook example of profiting from gambling. A no-position "matching" model is just what bookmaking/pool-selling, "promoting gambling" and "professional gambling" statutes were written to reach. The framing does not reduce liability. It shows the operator is not a player.

### Cited Findings
- NY Penal Law § 225.00(2): a person "engages in gambling when he stakes or risks something of value upon the outcome of a contest of chance or a future contingent event not under his control or influence…" — [NY Senate, PEN 225.00](https://nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PEN/225.00); [newyork.public.law § 225.00](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._penal_law_section_225.00)
- NY § 225.00(3) "player": a person who gambles only as a contestant or bettor, "without receiving or becoming entitled to receive any profit therefrom other than personal gambling winnings, and without otherwise rendering any material assistance to the establishment, conduct or operation of the particular gambling activity". The statute adds that a person who engages in "bookmaking" is not a "player". — [newyork.public.law § 225.00](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._penal_law_section_225.00); [Justia NY 225.00 (2006)](https://law.justia.com/codes/new-york/2006/penal/pen0225.00_225.00.html)
- NY § 225.00(4) "advances gambling activity": a person who, acting other than as a player, "materially aids any form of gambling activity". The statutory examples include establishing, conducting or operating it and "acceptance of money risked" (full wording per ypdcrime/Justia text). — [ypdcrime Art. 225](https://ypdcrime.com/penal.law/article225.php); [Justia 2006](https://law.justia.com/codes/new-york/2006/penal/pen0225.00_225.00.html)
- NY § 225.00(5) "profits from gambling activity": a person who, other than as a player, "accepts or receives money or other property pursuant to an agreement or understanding with any person whereby he participates or is to participate in the proceeds of gambling activity". — [NY Senate](https://nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PEN/225.00) (search excerpt truncated; full text at [newyork.public.law](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._penal_law_section_225.00))
- NY "bookmaking": advancing gambling activity by unlawfully accepting bets from members of the public *as a business*, rather than in a casual or personal fashion. — [newyork.public.law](https://newyork.public.law/laws/n.y._penal_law_section_225.00)
- NY promoting-gambling offenses are §§ 225.05 (second degree) and 225.10 (first degree). — [Promoting Gambling: Penal Law 225.05 & 225.10 (NY criminal law blog)](https://newyorkcriminallawyer-blog.com/promoting-gambling-and-related)
- New York's high court in *White v. Cuomo* (N.Y. Mar. 2022) read the "contest of chance" definition when it upheld the DFS statute. ### Inferences
- **Player vs operator.** Under the NY template, people who only stake money and collect their own winnings are "players" and face at most minor liability. NY does not criminalize simple player gambling at all. Anyone who "materially aids" gambling (running the platform, holding stakes, matching bets) or who "participates in the proceeds" (a 10% commission on winnings) is outside the player definition. That person is the target of promoting-gambling and bookmaking offenses. The founder's "I only match people and take no position" description lines up almost point by point with § 225.00(4)-(5). Holding escrowed stakes is "acceptance of money risked". The commission is "participating in the proceeds".
- **Rake = commercial operator.** States that tolerate social gambling (e.g., Texas Penal Code § 47.02(b) private-place defense; many "social gambling" exceptions) usually condition the exception on *no one receiving any economic benefit other than personal winnings* and on the players being on equal footing. A house rake or commission defeats every such exception. *(Texas § 47.02/47.03 wording is unverified this session. Check [Texas Penal Code ch. 47](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/PE/htm/PE.47.htm). From general knowledge: § 47.03 "gambling promotion", for gain, receiving/recording/forwarding bets or operating a gambling business, is a Class A misdemeanor. § 47.04 covers keeping a gambling place. § 47.05 covers communicating gambling information.)*
- **California** *(unverified this session; check [Penal Code 337a on leginfo](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PEN&sectionNum=337a.))*: From general knowledge, § 337a(a) criminalizes "pool selling or bookmaking", keeping a place for recording bets, receiving/holding/forwarding money bet "upon the result of any … contest … or upon the result of … any future contingent event", and acting as a stakeholder for such bets. A peer-to-peer exchange that holds stakes in escrow arguably fits the "stakeholder" prong exactly. § 337a is a wobbler for first offenses and carries higher penalties for repeats. § 330 (banking/percentage games) is mainly aimed at casino-style games. Its "percentage game" concept also targets a house taking a cut. AB 831 (2025) separately extended liability to suppliers and vendors of illegal online gambling (see Section 4).
- **Illinois** *(unverified this session; check [720 ILCS 5/Art. 28](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs4.asp?ActID=1876&ChapterID=53&SeqStart=68700000&SeqEnd=70000000))*: From general knowledge, § 28-1(a) gambling covers making a book on "the result of any political nomination, appointment or election" and accepting bets as a business. § 28-1.1 "syndicated gambling" is a felony aimed at bookmaking businesses that take bets for gain. § 28-3 covers keeping a gambling place. Election contracts are expressly named in the Illinois statute.
- **Washington** is the harshest: RCW 9.46.240 makes knowing internet transmission or receipt of gambling information a felony. Washington's "professional gambling" offenses (RCW 9.46.0269, 9.46.220-.222; unverified this session) apply the same "other than as a player / materially aids / accepts money pursuant to an agreement to participate in proceeds" test as New York.
- **Nevada**: NRS 465.092 reaches operators "within or outside of this state", so offshore or out-of-state hosting is no defense. Unlicensed operation of a gambling game/sports pool also violates NRS 463.160 (licensing requirement; unverified this session).
- An unregistered operator has no federal preemption argument. Kalshi and others defend on the ground that CFTC-designated contract markets trade "swaps" under exclusive CFTC jurisdiction (CEA § 2(a)(1)(A)). A site not registered with the CFTC cannot invoke that defense. Its contracts would, if anything, be *illegal* off-exchange swaps under federal law as well (federal exposure is outside this scope). Its state exposure is therefore exactly that of an offshore/unlicensed sportsbook.

- No source found addressing a P2P prediction-market operator specifically prosecuted (as opposed to Kalshi-type DCMs). Analogies are to offshore sportsbooks and bookmaking cases.

## 2. Elements of gambling, "dominant factor" vs "material element" tests, and whether event outcomes are "chance" / "future contingent events"

### Takeaway
The three elements are consideration, chance and prize. Real-money markets plainly satisfy consideration and prize. Elections, sports and economic data are "future contingent event[s] not under [the bettor's] control or influence" under the NY/MPC-style definitions used by NY, WA and many others. That alternative prong makes the skill-vs-chance debate largely irrelevant: forecasting skill does not convert an event bet into a lawful contest. Skill arguments help mainly for contests where the *participant's own performance* decides the result (e.g., DFS). Even there, results vary by state test.

### Cited Findings
- NY § 225.00(2) defines gambling disjunctively: staking something of value on "a contest of chance **or** a future contingent event not under his control or influence". — [NY Senate PEN 225.00](https://nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/PEN/225.00)
- Washington's RCW 9.46.0237 uses the same "contest of chance or a future contingent event not under the person's control or influence" construction (confirmed for 9.46.240's structure; 9.46.0237 text not retrieved this session). — [RCW 9.46 (official)](https://apps.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=9.46.240)
- Most states use either the material element test (gambling if chance is a *material* element, even if not dominant) or the dominant/predominant factor test (not gambling if skill predominates). A few use an "any chance" test. — [Mondaq/Klein Moynihan, Games of Skill v. Games of Chance](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/gaming/730092/games-of-skill-v-games-of-chance-the-legal-analysis); [Wikipedia, Dominant Factor Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominant_Factor_Test); [Skadden DFS memo 2020](https://www.skadden.com/-/media/files/publications/2020/08/dailyfantasysportsdecisionsriskcloudinglegallandsc.pdf)
- The material-element test finds a "game of chance" where chance is material but not dominant. Washington and New Jersey are cited as material-element examples. — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/gaming/730092/games-of-skill-v-games-of-chance-the-legal-analysis); [firstamendment.com skill gaming guide](https://www.firstamendment.com/skill-gaming-legal-guide/)
- Illinois Supreme Court (2020, *Dew-Becker v. Wu*) held head-to-head DFS is predominantly skill and rejected a gambling-loss-recovery claim. This is an example of a skill defense working, but only for contests decided by participants' own roster choices. — [Duane Morris alert](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/illinois_supreme_court_finds_daily_fantasy_sports_legal_rejects_dfs_losers_gambling_loss_0520.html); [Foley](https://www.foley.com/?p=48789)
- Courts in the Kalshi litigation have characterized sports event contracts as gambling in substance. The Ninth Circuit (Aug. 28, 2026) reportedly said the contracts are "sports gambling, regardless of whether Kalshi calls them swaps". — [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/25/us-court-rules-against-kalshi-says-states-can-regulate-prediction-markets); [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/tech-and-telecom-law/kalshi-takes-hit-as-appeals-court-says-sports-bets-arent-swaps)
- Arizona's charges specifically include four counts on *election* contracts (2028 presidential race, 2026 AZ gubernatorial race and primary, 2026 AZ Secretary of State). Arizona separately bars betting on elections. — [CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/policy/2026/03/17/arizona-attorney-general-charges-kalshi-with-illegal-gambling-election-betting); [Arizona Mirror](https://azmirror.com/2026/03/17/in-unprecedented-move-arizona-files-criminal-charges-against-prediction-market-company-kalshi/)

### Inferences
- **The "future contingent event" prong ends the skill argument.** A bettor forecasting an election, an economic print, a weather event or a game does not control or influence the outcome. The NY/WA definitions therefore apply regardless of how skillful the forecaster is. The skill-vs-chance tests matter only in states whose definitions turn solely on a "game/contest of chance". Even under a dominant-factor test, courts generally treat the *outcome of an event* as the chance element from the bettor's perspective, unlike DFS, where participants assemble rosters.
- **Market structure does not change the elements.** An order book, $1 binary contracts and price discovery do not remove consideration (the stake), prize ($1 payout) or the contingent outcome. Three federal circuits (Ninth, Sixth; and implicitly every state court so far) analyzed CFTC-regulated contracts on their *substance* as bets. An unregulated site has a weaker case still.
- **User-created markets add risk.** Markets on personal or culture events where a participant *can* influence the outcome (e.g., "Will I finish my marathon?") create match-fixing and fraud issues. Where the *creator* controls the event, the market may fall outside "not under his control" for that person while remaining gambling for every counterparty.

### Gaps
- No court decision found that classifies a *real-money* prediction market on non-sports events (economics, weather) as lawful skill gaming under state law.
- Precise state-by-state assignment of dominant vs material vs any-chance tests is inconsistent across secondary sources. Not verified per state.

## 3. 2025-2026 state actions against prediction markets (status as of Nov. 13, 2026)

### Takeaway
States and tribes are winning most of the litigation even against CFTC-registered exchanges. The Ninth Circuit (Nevada, Aug. 28, 2026; en banc rehearing reportedly denied) and Sixth Circuit (Ohio/Tennessee, Sept. 25, 2026) held sports event contracts are not "swaps" and/or that state gambling law is not preempted. The Third Circuit (NJ, Apr. 6, 2026, 2-1) went the other way, creating a circuit split now before the Supreme Court (cert petitions pending; no grant found). Massachusetts won a state-court injunction (stayed on appeal), Arizona filed criminal charges, and Nevada sued Polymarket in state court. If DCM-registered Kalshi, Robinhood and Crypto.com face these results, an unregistered site has no preemption shield at all.

## 4. Social / sweepstakes models and play-money prediction markets

### Takeaway
"Sweepstakes" dual-currency workarounds were outlawed by statute in a growing list of states in 2025-2026 (CA AB 831 effective Jan. 1, 2026; NY S5935; NJ; CT; MT; others), and NY's AG took action against 26 platforms. Manifold Markets, the best-known play-money prediction market, abandoned its real-money "sweepcash" in March 2025 citing the regulatory landscape. A pure free-to-play, no-purchase-required and no-cash-out site removes the "something of value" (consideration) and "prize" elements. It is generally lawful, but it is lawful *because* it gives up the real-money model, and any cash-out, prize or charity-redemption feature reopens risk.

### Cited Findings
- California AB 831 was signed by Gov. Newsom on Oct. 11, 2025 and took effect Jan. 1, 2026. It bans online sweepstakes casinos using dual-currency systems that simulate gambling for cash or cash equivalents, extends liability to vendors, suppliers and payment processors, and is enforceable criminally (Penal Code) and civilly (B&P Code). It passed both chambers unanimously. — [ZwillGen](https://www.zwillgen.com/gaming/californias-ab-831-bans-sweepstakes-casinos-expands-liability-vendors/); [Frankfurt Kurnit (FKKS)](https://advertisinglaw.fkks.com/post/102lrox/california-bans-online-sweepstakes-casinos); [CasinoBeats](https://casinobeats.com/2025/10/13/california-sweepstakes-casino-ban-ab-831/)
- New York S5935 sweepstakes ban was signed by Gov. Hochul (signing/effective date reported as Dec. 2025; sources conflict). The NY AG acted against 26 sweepstakes platforms, all of which stopped sweepstakes-coin sales in NY. — [iGaming Business 2025 year in review](https://igamingbusiness.com/legal-compliance/2025-sweepstakes-casinos-year-in-review/); [track360 tracker](https://track360.io/blog/states-banning-sweepstakes-casinos-2026-legislative-tracker-operator-playbook)
- One tracker (Aug. 2026) lists hard statutory dual-currency bans in force in California (AB 831), Connecticut (SB 1235), Indiana (HB 1052), Maine (LD 2007), Montana (SB 555), New Jersey (A5447), New York (S5935A / Ch. 605), and Tennessee (SB 2136). Other trackers count up to eleven states, and Nevada is also listed among 2025 enactments. Counts conflict. — [track360](https://track360.io/blog/states-banning-sweepstakes-casinos-2026-legislative-tracker-operator-playbook); [Deuces Cracked roundup](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/sweepstakes-casino-bans-2026-state-by-state-roundup); [Tribal Gaming (June 2026)](https://tribalgaming.com/news/sweepstakes-casino-bans-tribal-exclusivity-2026/)
- Kentucky and Florida have sued sweepstakes operator VGW. — [Sweepsy](https://www.sweepsy.com/?p=26344)
- Manifold Markets' March 2025 investor update says it shut down its sweepstakes ("sweepcash") and returned to play money. Reasons: the "regulatory and competitive landscape has changed significantly" and sweepstakes made the product "more complex and confusing". The sweepcash program reportedly ran Sept. 2024 to Mar. 28, 2025. One source says April 2025. — [Manifold investor update, 3/6/25 (primary)](https://manifoldmarkets.notion.site/Investor-update-3-6-25-1ac54492ea7a8022aaf1ed2cd188663d); [tech-insider review](https://tech-insider.org/manifold-markets-review/)
- Manifold had geo-restricted sweepcash in Idaho, Delaware, Michigan and Washington. — [predictionmarketsworld Idaho page](https://predictionmarketsworld.pages.dev/en-us/states/idaho/)
- Manifold's "mana" is now play money with no cash-out path. The only off-platform use reported is charity donation at about M100 per $1. — [pm.wiki explainer](https://pm.wiki/sv/learn/manifold-markets-explained); [Astral Codex Ten, Mantic Monday 5/13/24](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/mantic-monday-51324)
- Prophet Exchange left NJ real-money exchange betting in May 2024 and relaunched as ProphetX with a *sweepstakes* sportsbook. It later obtained CFTC approval as a prediction market, which is an example of sweepstakes as a transitional model. — [Covers, July 30, 2024](https://www.covers.com/industry/prophet-exchange-to-relaunch-as-prophet-x-with-sweepstakes-sportsbook-game-july-30-2024); [Gambling Insider](https://www.gamblinginsider.com/news/166600/prophetx-gets-cftc-approval-to-operate-prediction-market)

### Inferences
- **Free-to-play with no cash-out.** If users pay nothing of value to play and can win nothing of value (no cash, prizes, crypto or transferable tokens), the consideration and prize elements are missing, and the site falls outside the gambling definitions above in essentially all states. Risk returns if: (a) users can buy play currency *and* redeem anything of value (the exact dual-currency pattern now banned in CA, NY, NJ and others); (b) leaderboards award prizes (sweepstakes/contest law: no-purchase-necessary rules, registration and bonding in NY/FL for prizes over $5,000); (c) play currency is tradable on secondary markets; or (d) "charity redemption" is used. Charity redemption is untested and arguably lower risk since the user receives nothing, but no authority confirms this.
- The founder's 10%-commission, real-money model cannot be rescued by relabeling credits as "sweepstakes coins" in the states that have now banned dual-currency models. Elsewhere sweepstakes defenses are under heavy AG pressure.

### Gaps
- No primary statute text was retrieved for NY S5935, NJ A5447, CT SB 1235 or MT SB 555. Effective dates need verification on official legislature sites.
- No authority found holding that a pure play-money prediction market is illegal anywhere. Equally, no affirmative state ruling blessing one.

## 5. Civil exposure: gambling-loss recovery statutes, AG civil actions, private suits

### Takeaway
Many states keep centuries-old "loss recovery" statutes that let losers, and after a waiting period any person or the state, sue operators/winners/stakeholders for losses, often trebled. Kentucky used its statute to win a nine-figure judgment against PokerStars' owner, Kentuckians have used it against Kalshi, and AGs are bringing civil injunction suits (MA, NV). An escrow-holding, commission-taking operator is a natural "stakeholder"/"winner" defendant. These statutes reach every losing bet, so exposure scales with gross losing volume rather than with the operator's 10% revenue.

### Cited Findings
- Kentucky Loss Recovery Act (KRS 372.020, 372.040): a loser has six months to sue to recover losses from illegal gambling. After that, any person may sue for **treble** the losses, going back five years. — [WKYU / LPM](https://www.wkyufm.org/2024-10-04/lawsuit-seeks-to-recover-all-kentuckians-gambling-losses-from-gray-machines); [ABA Journal](https://abajournal.com/news/article/ky._cites_obscure_law_in_suit_to_recover_residents_online_gambling_losses)
- *Commonwealth v. Pocket Kings* (PokerStars): filed 2010 in Franklin Circuit Court. The trial court entered a judgment of about $870 million (later reported as much higher with interest). The Court of Appeals reversed in Dec. 2018 (state not a "person"). In 2020 the Kentucky Supreme Court reversed and reinstated the Commonwealth's standing and judgment. A later report describes the resolution as a roughly $300 million recovery. — [Courthouse News (2018)](https://www.courthousenews.com/kentucky-court-upends-870m-pokerstars-judgment/); [Washington Times/AP (2018)](https://m.washingtontimes.com/news/2018/dec/21/court-zaps-kentuckys-870-million-win-against-onlin/); [KY Supreme Court 2020 opinion summary](https://hallapproved.com/ky/cases/supreme/2020/4840244/); [Insurance Journal (2021)](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/southeast/2021/01/20/597975.htm); [Gambling and the Law](https://www.gamblingandthelaw.com/column/kentucky-sues-to-recover-poker-losses/)
- Kentuckians filed a federal suit against Kalshi seeking to recover gambling losses, alleging its sports, election and weather contracts are illegal under Kentucky law. — [CDC Gaming](https://cdcgaming.com/brief/kentuckians-sue-kalshi-to-recover-gambling-losses/)
- A 2024 nonprofit suit under the same Kentucky statute seeks treble losses of all gray-machine gamblers. — [Public Gaming](https://publicgaming.com/news-categories/legal/13370-kentucky-lawsuit-seeks-triple-the-losses-of-all-gray-machine-gamblers); [GGB News](https://ggbnews.com/article/judge-rules-that-kentucky-gamblers-can-sue-to-recover-losses)
- Illinois' loss-recovery statute was the vehicle in *Dew-Becker v. Wu* (Ill. 2020). The claim failed only because DFS was found to be skill. — [Duane Morris](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/illinois_supreme_court_finds_daily_fantasy_sports_legal_rejects_dfs_losers_gambling_loss_0520.html)
- Massachusetts AG civil enforcement against Kalshi produced a PI (Jan. 2026, stayed on appeal). Nevada's regulator obtained a TRO against Polymarket in a civil enforcement action (Jan.-Feb. 2026). — [Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-secures-court-order-that-will-block-kalshi-from-offering-unlawful-sports-wagers-in-massachusetts); [SBC Americas](https://sbcamericas.com/2026/02/02/nevada-judge-initial-ban-polymarket/)

### Inferences
- Other loss-recovery statutes from general knowledge (unverified this session; confirm on official sites): NY General Obligations Law § 5-421 (loser may sue the winner or *stakeholder*; if not within three months, others may sue); Massachusetts G.L. c. 137 § 1 (loser may recover within three months; thereafter any person may sue for treble); Illinois 720 ILCS 5/28-8 (losses of $50 or more recoverable, with treble actions by third parties after six months); similar statutes exist in Georgia, South Carolina, Ohio, New Jersey (N.J.S.A. 2A:40-5 et seq.) and others. NY GOL § 5-401/5-411 also make wagering contracts void.
- Because the platform holds escrowed stakes and pays winners, it is the obvious "stakeholder" defendant under NY-style statutes. Class actions aggregating all losing users in a state, plus third-party treble "qui tam"-like suits (KY model), could produce liability many times the operator's commission revenue.
- Other civil theories: state UDAP/consumer-protection claims, unjust enrichment, AG injunctions, and asset forfeiture of escrowed funds as gambling proceeds.

- Status of the Kentuckians-v.-Kalshi loss-recovery suit not found.

## 6. Which states license peer-to-peer betting exchanges / exchange wagering, and what licensing involves

### Takeaway
State-licensed P2P exchange wagering exists but is very limited. New Jersey licensed the first US sports betting exchanges (Prophet Exchange, Aug. 2022; Sporttrade, Sept. 2022) under its DGE sports wagering regime. Prophet exited NJ in May 2024. Sporttrade has been the sole NJ exchange and has since exited online sports betting while it seeks CFTC DCM status. Sporttrade's Colorado and Iowa presence offered only conventional sports betting. No state was found that licenses a general-purpose (politics, economics, culture) user-created event exchange. Lawful operation would require full sportsbook-style licensure in each state (and in most states, event categories like elections are not permitted at all), or CFTC registration.

### Cited Findings
- Prophet Exchange was the first regulated P2P sports betting exchange in the US. It went live (Aug. 2022) after approval from the New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement. — [iGaming Business](https://igamingbusiness.com/legal-compliance/legal/prophet-launches-first-us-regulated-sports-betting-exchange); [GGB News](https://ggbnews.com/article/new-jersey-grants-first-peer-to-peer-sports-betting-exchange-in-u-s); [Yogonet, Aug. 29, 2022](https://www.yogonet.com/international/news/2022/08/29/64020-prophet-exchange-launches-in-nj-as-first-legal-us-sports-betting-exchange-former-giants-star-victor-cruz-places-inaugural-bet)
- Prophet stopped taking bets May 15 and exited NJ May 31 (2024). It relaunched as ProphetX (sweepstakes) and later won CFTC approval for a sports prediction exchange. — [Legal Sports Report](https://www.legalsportsreport.com/183374/prophet-exchange-offline-in-nj-sports-betting-market/); [Covers](https://www.covers.com/industry/prophet-exchange-to-relaunch-as-prophet-x-with-sweepstakes-sportsbook-game-july-30-2024); [Gambling Insider](https://www.gamblinginsider.com/news/166600/prophetx-gets-cftc-approval-to-operate-prediction-market)
- Sporttrade launched about a month after Prophet and became the sole remaining NJ exchange. It is also live in Colorado and Iowa but offers only traditional sports betting there. Per later reporting, it exited online sports betting with DCM applications pending before the CFTC. New Jersey is described as "the only state to have legalized the betting exchange model". Liquidity is a barrier because exchanges can only take in-state action. — [Legal Sports Report](https://www.legalsportsreport.com/183374/prophet-exchange-offline-in-nj-sports-betting-market/)
- Prophet originally announced plans for both NJ and Indiana. — [Casino.org](https://www.casino.org/news/new-to-us-prophet-to-launch-sports-betting-exchanges-in-nj-and-indiana/)
- Illinois reportedly enacted SB 3019 (June 2026) creating a licensing/tax regime for prediction markets. This is unverified and conflicts with other reports of an Illinois bill to *prohibit* sports trades. — [ticker-tracker Illinois](https://ticker-tracker.com/legal/illinois); [NEXT.io](https://next.io/news/regulation/illinois-prediction-markets-bill-seeks-prohibit-sports-trades/)
- Massachusetts' court order conditions Kalshi's sports offerings on Massachusetts Gaming Commission licensure, which shows the state route is a sports-wagering license. — [Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/news/ag-campbell-secures-court-order-that-will-block-kalshi-from-offering-unlawful-sports-wagers-in-massachusetts)

### Inferences
- What licensing involves (general knowledge of state sports-wagering regimes; unverified this session): an operator license or a partnership with a land-based casino/racetrack licensee in most states; suitability investigations of owners and key employees; multi-hundred-thousand to million-dollar license fees; GLI-certified platforms; geolocation; KYC/AML; responsible-gaming tools; age 21+ in most states; segregated player funds/bonding; DGE/commission approval of each event type (sports and limited approved events only, typically excluding elections, and in many states excluding college props); and state taxes on gross gaming revenue (for an exchange, the commission). Nevada licenses exchanges in principle under NRS 463/Gaming Commission regulations, but I found no operating Nevada P2P exchange.
- No state licenses a user-created, any-topic event market. The founder's model (politics, culture, economics, user-generated markets) therefore has no licensing path in any state. The only US route for non-sports events is CFTC DCM registration, and even that is now contested by states as shown in Section 3.
- The task's premise that Sporttrade is licensed in Arizona and Virginia was not confirmed. No source found supports exchange licensing in AZ or VA.
