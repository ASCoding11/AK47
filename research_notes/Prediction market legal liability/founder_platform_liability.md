## 1. Founder personal liability (criminal and civil); does an LLC or C-corp help?

### Takeaway
A corporate entity limits a founder's exposure to contract claims and ordinary business debts. It does not shield a founder from their own criminal acts, from statutes that name the person who "conducts, manages, supervises, directs or owns" an illegal gambling business, from CEA aiding-and-abetting or controlling-person liability, or from personal BSA/AML charges. Recent practice cuts both ways. Founders have been raided and convicted (Polymarket's Coplan raid in 2024; BitMEX guilty pleas in 2022). They have also had probes dropped or been pardoned (Polymarket probes closed in July 2025; BitMEX founders pardoned in March 2025). Enforcement intensity depends heavily on politics, and that can change with a new administration.

### Cited Findings
- 18 U.S.C. 1955 makes it a federal felony for "whoever conducts, finances, manages, supervises, directs, or owns all or part of an illegal gambling business". That is personal liability that attaches to individuals by the statute's own terms, regardless of corporate form — [18 U.S.C. 1955, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1955)
- CEA Section 13(b) (7 U.S.C. 13c(b)): a person who directly or indirectly controls a violator is liable as a principal unless they acted in good faith and did not induce the violation. CEA 13(a) (7 U.S.C. 13c(a)) imposes aiding-and-abetting liability. Section 2(a)(1)(B) makes an entity liable for its agents' acts — [7 U.S.C. 13c, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/13c)
- Responsible corporate officer doctrine: under United States v. Park, 421 U.S. 658 (1975), an officer with authority to prevent or correct a violation can be criminally liable without proving personal intent. The doctrine is confined mainly to public-welfare offenses such as the FDCA. Gambling, BSA and CEA fraud charges usually require personal participation or willfulness, which a hands-on founder of a small platform will often meet anyway — [United States v. Park, Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/421/658/)
- Polymarket: the CFTC settled with Polymarket in January 2022 for operating an unregistered binary-options facility ($1.4M penalty; agreed to block US users) — [SBC Americas](https://sbcamericas.com/2025/07/15/polymarket-no-longer-investigated/)
- In November 2024 the FBI searched CEO Shayne Coplan's home and seized his phone and electronics. The criminal probe was run by the Manhattan U.S. Attorney's Office and examined whether Polymarket kept accepting US users through VPNs — [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/justice-department-ends-investigation-polymarket); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/330214/polymarket-investigations-dropped-justice-department-cftc?amp=1)
- In early July 2025 the DOJ and CFTC each told Polymarket it was no longer under investigation; Bloomberg reported this on July 15, 2025. No charges were filed — [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-07-15/polymarket-probe-ended-by-doj-in-win-for-crypto-bets-under-trump); [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/regulation/2025-07-15-us-justice-department-and-cftc-close-probes-into-polymarket-bloomberg-362703)
- BitMEX: co-founders Arthur Hayes, Benjamin Delo and Samuel Reed were charged in 2020 and pleaded guilty in 2022 to willfully violating the Bank Secrecy Act from 2015 to 2020 by failing to maintain AML and KYC programs. Reported terms were $10M forfeiture each and sentences of probation or house arrest (Hayes: 6 months home detention plus 2 years probation; Delo: 30 months probation; Reed: 18 months probation; employee Gregory Dwyer: 12 months probation). Separately there was a CFTC settlement; reports of the amounts conflict ($10M each vs. $30M combined) — [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-03-28/trump-pardons-bitmex-co-founders-arthur-hayes-benjamin-delo-and-samuel-reed); [CoinMarketCap Academy](https://coinmarketcap.com/academy/article/trump-pardons-3-bitmex-co-founders-following-guilty-pleas-in-money-laundering-case)
- President Trump pardoned Hayes, Delo and Reed (and reportedly Dwyer) on March 28, 2025 — [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/348719/trump-pardons-bitmex-co-founders-including-arthur-hayes-report); [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/donald-trump-pardon-bitmex-founders)
- The BitMEX lesson: the founders were convicted personally for compliance-program failures (not having KYC/AML), not for fraud. Design choices such as skipping KYC can be crimes for individual founders — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/crypto/trump-pardons-bitmex-crypto-exchange-co-founder-arthur-hayes)
- Intrade: the CFTC sued Intrade and Trade Exchange Network (Ireland) in November 2012 for offering off-exchange options to US persons. Founder John Delaney had died in 2011 before the suit, so there was no personal action against him; Intrade shut down in 2013. [background, not re-verified this session; check CFTC press release archive at https://www.cftc.gov/PressRoom/PressReleases]
- FTX: Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud and conspiracy in November 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in 2024. This shows that commingling customer escrowed funds is a personal-fraud risk. [background, not re-verified this session; see DOJ SDNY press releases]
- State AGs are also suing operators. New York AG Letitia James sued Kalshi on July 31, 2026 in Manhattan state court, seeking an injunction, restitution, forfeiture, treble damages and a $100,000 penalty per solicitation, which the AG calculated at $36B. Kalshi sought to remove the case to federal court. The AG brought similar suits against Coinbase and Gemini in April 2026. These suits name the entities; the reports did not mention individual defendants — [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/410247/new-york-sues-kalshi); [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/litigation/new-york-sues-kalshi-for-running-illegal-gambling-operation); [NY1](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/news/2026/07/31/new-york-sues-kalshi--alleging-illegal-gambling-operation-)

### Inferences
- For this founder, an LLC or C-corp helps with contract and tort claims from users, with vendor debt, and with civil judgments against the entity, provided the founder keeps corporate formalities and does not commingle funds. It does not help with criminal liability under §1955, state gambling laws, BSA, CEA aiding-and-abetting or controlling-person liability, or wire fraud. A sole founder who personally designs, lists and resolves markets and sets the 10% rake is the textbook "person who conducts, manages, ... owns" the business.
- Escrowing user stakes increases personal exposure if the funds are commingled or misused (the FTX pattern) and supports money-transmitter theories. Segregated, third-party-held accounts reduce that exposure.
- Polymarket's survival depended on offshore structuring, geo-blocking, a 2022 settlement and a favorable 2025 political climate. Its closures carry no precedential value for a small US-based operator.
- The "mostly a marketplace" framing (never taking a side, only taking fees) is legally weak against state "bookmaking" and "promoting gambling" theories, which typically cover anyone who profits from facilitating bets. A rake on winnings is the classic gambling-house "vig".

### Gaps
- I did not find an example of a US founder personally prosecuted under §1955 for a prediction market specifically. Case examples come from offshore sportsbooks (e.g., 2006 BetonSports/World Sports Exchange prosecutions [background, not verified]).
- I could not verify the exact BitMEX CFTC penalty allocation.

## 2. Section 230 (47 U.S.C. 230) for user-created markets

### Takeaway
Section 230 is unlikely to protect the operator in any meaningful way. It expressly does not affect federal criminal law. Courts treat liability that arises from the platform's own conduct (processing payments, completing transactions, collecting fees, resolving outcomes) as outside the immunity. At most it may help against claims that treat the platform as the publisher of a user's offensive market title or description.

### Cited Findings
- 47 U.S.C. 230(e)(1): "Nothing in this section shall be construed to impair the enforcement of ... any ... Federal criminal statute" — [47 U.S.C. 230, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230)
- HomeAway.com v. City of Santa Monica, 918 F.3d 676 (9th Cir. 2019): the Ninth Circuit unanimously upheld an ordinance barring platforms from completing booking transactions for unregistered properties. It held that the duty concerns the platform's own transactions, which are "distinct, internal, and nonpublic", not the publication of third-party listings, so Section 230 did not preempt the ordinance — [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2019/05/ninth-circuit-chunks-another-section-230-ruling-homeaway-v-santa-monica-catch-up-post.htm); [Leagle](https://www.leagle.com/decision/infco20190313140); [Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/articles/20190314/19312341797/ninth-circuit-tells-online-services-section-230-isnt-you.shtml?cid=321)
- Turo v. City of Los Angeles (C.D. Cal. 2020) relied on HomeAway and Force v. Facebook to hold that Section 230 turns on the function the defendant performs, not on its identity — [Harvard TagTeam/Goldman](https://tagteam.harvard.edu/hub_feeds/3624/feed_items/2748447)
- Social casino litigation (In re Apple Inc. App Store Simulated Casino-Style Games Litig., N.D. Cal.): in September 2022 Judge Davila held that Section 230 did not protect Apple, Google and Meta for processing payments for virtual chips in casino-style apps, even though the apps were third-party content — [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2022/09/section-230-doesnt-protect-app-stores-that-sell-virtual-chips-for-casino-apps-in-re-apple-app-store.htm)
- Procedural history: the Ninth Circuit heard an interlocutory appeal in April 2024 but did not decide the Section 230 question on the merits ("punted"). After remand, the district court ruled again. New appeals (Nos. 25-7916 Google, 25-7917 Apple) were pending in 2026, with EFF and CCIA amicus briefs (April 2026) urging reversal of the payment-processing holding — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/396244/appeals-court-punts-on-online-gamblers-suits-agai.html); [EFF 2026](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/04/eff-9th-circuit-again-app-stores-shouldnt-be-liable-processing-payments-user); [Apple opening brief](https://epic.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Apple-Opening-Brief.pdf); [CCIA brief](https://ccianet.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/LITIG-Casino-App-Custadero-Apple-Brief.pdf)

### Inferences
- This operator matches, escrows, resolves and takes 10% of winnings. That makes it the "information content provider" and transaction party for every bet, a much stronger case against immunity than the app stores. Even if the Ninth Circuit reverses in the social-casino appeal, the reversal would concern intermediaries that only process payments, not the operator of the gambling game itself.
- Resolving markets is the platform's own conduct, not publication of third-party content, so resolution-dispute claims (Section 4) fall outside Section 230.
- Section 230 is not a defense to state AG gambling suits or federal criminal charges. It might bar a defamation or harassment claim arising from a user-written market title about a private person, unless the platform materially contributed, for example by editing, approving or promoting the market.

### Gaps
- Status of Nos. 25-7916/25-7917 as of November 2026 (whether argued or decided) is unknown.
- I found no court decision applying Section 230 to a prediction market specifically.

## 3. Harmful markets (death, assassination, war, terrorism, crimes, private individuals or minors)

### Takeaway
User-created markets on deaths, attacks, wars or crimes create the highest-severity risks. These include possible criminal conspiracy or solicitation theories, CFTC public-interest prohibitions under CEA 5c(c)(5)(C) and the June 2026 proposed Rule 40.11 amendments, congressional and senatorial pressure, payment-processor and app-store termination, and settlement-design lawsuits (the Kalshi Khamenei case). Even the large regulated players remove such markets reactively, and that removal itself produces litigation when it changes payouts.

### Cited Findings
- CEA Section 5c(c)(5)(C) (7 U.S.C. 7a-2(c)(5)(C)) lets the CFTC prohibit event contracts involving terrorism, assassination, war, gaming or activity unlawful under federal or state law if they are contrary to the public interest. 17 CFR 40.11 bars registered entities from listing such contracts — [7 U.S.C. 7a-2, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/7a-2); [17 CFR 40.11, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/40.11)
- On June 10, 2026 the CFTC proposed amending Rule 40.11 and adding Appendix F (comments due July 27, 2026). The proposal reads terrorism, assassination and war broadly: a contract "involves" them if they could trigger settlement (e.g., a foreign leader leaving office by a date), unless it expressly excludes those outcomes. Such contracts are "highly likely" to be contrary to the public interest. It adds a definition of "gaming"; contracts on aggregate crime rates or trial outcomes are more likely to be permitted; sports contracts largely continue. As of these notes it is a proposal, not a final rule — [WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20260618-10-takeaways-from-the-cftc-event-contracts-proposed-rulemaking); [Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/06/cftc-issues-notice-of-proposed-rulemaking); [CRS LSB11441](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11441.html); [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/regulation/2026-06-10-cftc-unveils-sweeping-rule-proposal-fast-growing-prediction-markets-404258)
- Augur (July 2018): within days of launch, user-created markets on whether Donald Trump, Warren Buffett, John McCain or Jeff Bezos would die or be killed appeared on the decentralized platform. Augur's front-end aggregators censored them, but the protocol could not remove them. Commentators said the market creators faced the most immediate legal risk — [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2018/07/26/2449/the-latest-blockchain-use-case-anonymously-betting-on-public-figure-death-pools/); [CoinDesk](https://dev.coindesk.com/markets/2018/07/25/the-first-augur-assassination-markets-have-arrived); [Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2018/07/27/496202.htm); [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/blockchain-based-betting-platform-augur-now-features-assassination-markets)
- Polymarket removed a "nuclear detonation in 2026" market (at least $650K volume) in March 2026 after backlash, without a formal statement, having earlier tweeted a 22% probability — [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/news/ecosystems/2026-03-04-polymarket-removes-nuclear-detonation-market-392161); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/359897/polymarket-pulls-nuclear-detonation-market-following-public-backlash)
- In April 2026 Polymarket apologized for and removed a market on the fate of US pilots downed in the Iran conflict; a critic counted more than 200 war-related markets on the platform — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/04/06/polymarket-apology-market-downed-f-15-us-pilot-insider-trading-iran-war/); [Cointelegraph Magazine](https://cointelegraph.com/magazine/anger-grows-polymarket-bets-iran-war)
- Several senators urged regulators to curb markets tied to death, war or geopolitical events (spring 2026) — [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/359897/polymarket-pulls-nuclear-detonation-market-following-public-backlash)
- Kalshi's "Ali Khamenei out as Supreme Leader?" market (about $54M) settled at the last traded price under a death carve-out after Khamenei died (trading halted February 28, 2026). A class action followed: Risch v. KalshiEX LLC, No. 2:26-cv-02390 (C.D. Cal. filed March 5, 2026), alleging breach of contract and California consumer-law violations because the carve-out was not adequately disclosed. Kalshi said "we don't list markets directly tied to death", designs rules "to prevent people from profiting from death", and reimbursed fees and net losses — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bloomberg-government-news/kalshi-sued-over-death-carveout-in-iran-leader-prediction-market); [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/360293/kalshi-sued-refusing-pay-out-prediction-market-iran-leader-death); [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/kalshi-sued-khamenei-trade-carveout)
- The CFTC's January 2026 Van Dyke case shows the harm vector directly: a service member who planned the Maduro capture operation allegedly traded Polymarket contracts on it (see Section 4) — [Mondo Visione (CFTC release 9217-26)](https://mondovisione.com/news/cftc-charges-us-service-member-with-insider-trading-in-nicols-maduro-related-2026424/)

### Inferences
- Legal theories a prosecutor could use against a platform that lists a death or crime market: conspiracy or aiding and abetting (18 U.S.C. 2, 371) if the operator knows a market is being used to fund or reward a crime. Solicitation of a crime of violence (18 U.S.C. 373) mainly reaches the market creator or funder, but an operator that knowingly keeps a "bounty"-style market up risks complicity. These are my analytical inferences; I found no prosecution of a platform on these theories.
- Markets about private individuals or minors add defamation, privacy (intrusion or false light), harassment and stalking risk. Section 230 might cover pure user text, but not if the operator approves or curates the market (Section 2).
- Design choices that reduce risk: pre-listing human review and an approval queue; a published prohibited-topics policy (deaths, injuries, assassinations, terrorism, acts of war, crimes against identifiable people, private individuals, minors, anything a single participant could cause); explicit death or violence carve-outs disclosed prominently at the point of trade (the Khamenei lesson); and kill-switch and void-and-refund rules written into the terms before launch. Fully permissionless creation (the Augur model) increases risk.
- Reputational and processor risk: card networks and processors generally require gambling merchants to be licensed and coded correctly, and controversial markets accelerate account termination. [inference; I did not find a specific source on processor terminations for prediction markets]

### Gaps
- I found no criminal case against a platform operator for hosting an assassination or death market.
- I did not find documented payment-processor or app-store terminations of a prediction market over content.
- I did not verify Kalshi's specific rejected or withdrawn CFTC product filings (e.g., its earlier war or "political control" contracts).

## 4. Insider trading and manipulation

### Takeaway
On CFTC-regulated markets, CEA 6(c)(1) and Rule 180.1 (fraud-based manipulation, including misappropriation of confidential information) and CEA 9 now clearly reach event-contract insider trading. In 2026 the CFTC and DOJ brought the first cases, and the CFTC issued a prediction-markets enforcement advisory. Kalshi built an internal surveillance and discipline program, and the CFTC credits it. An unregistered P2P platform has no DCM self-regulatory duties, but it can still be a venue for fraud (wire fraud, CEA fraud if the contracts are swaps or options), and if it ignores or facilitates manipulation (including manipulation of its own resolution process) it faces aiding-and-abetting, consumer-protection and contract exposure. Congress has a wave of 2026 insider-trading bills, mostly aimed at officials; none had passed as of the last coverage I found.

### Cited Findings
- 7 U.S.C. 9 (CEA 6(c)(1)) and 17 CFR 180.1 prohibit any manipulative or deceptive device in connection with any swap, contract of sale of any commodity, or contract for future delivery — [7 U.S.C. 9, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/9); [17 CFR 180.1, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/180.1)
- February 25, 2026: Kalshi announced its first insider-trading enforcement actions, against a MrBeast/YouTube video editor and a political candidate who traded on his own race (5-year ban plus penalty). The same day, the CFTC Division of Enforcement issued a Prediction Markets Advisory stating that the Commission has full authority to police such conduct even when a DCM handles it internally — [Lowenstein Sandler](https://lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/cftc-and-kalshi-announce-enforcement-actions-targeting-prediction-markets-fctm); [JD Supra](https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/cftc-and-kalshi-announce-enforcement-9149599/); [Covers](https://www.covers.com/industry/kalshi-penalizes-politician-and-youtube-editor-for-insider-trading-violations-feb-25-2026)
- April 2026: Kalshi reportedly fined three political candidates a total of $7,552 and suspended them for 5 years for trading on their own elections (single aggregator source; verify) — [pulse.bot aggregator](https://www.pulse.bot/options-derivatives/news/kalshi-fines-three-politicians-7552-suspends-them-five-years-for-election-insider-trading-f2a00d53-88c3-4612-b269-112dfc559388/)
- April 23, 2026: CFTC v. Van Dyke, No. 26-cv-3369 (S.D.N.Y.). This was the first CFTC event-contract insider-trading case and the first use of the "Eddie Murphy rule" (CEA 4c(a)(4), misuse of nonpublic government information). An active-duty Army service member involved in planning "Operation Absolute Resolve" (the Maduro capture) allegedly made more than $404K trading Polymarket contracts. SDNY unsealed a parallel criminal indictment (wire fraud and related counts). These are allegations only — [Mondo Visione (CFTC release)](https://mondovisione.com/news/cftc-charges-us-service-member-with-insider-trading-in-nicols-maduro-related-2026424/); [Baker Botts](https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2026/april/cftc-brings-first-of-its-kind-insider-trading-action); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-us/insights/publications/2026/05/doj-and-cftc-criminal-and-civil-insider-trading-charges)
- August 2026: a former White House teleprompter operator was ordered to pay about $172,000 for trading Kalshi "mention" markets, with a 3-year trading ban. The CFTC credited Kalshi's surveillance for detecting it. This was the CFTC's second insider case against a federal employee trading event contracts — [The Block](https://theblock.co/news/regulation/2026-08-29-former-white-house-teleprompter-operator-ordered-to-pay-172000-for-kalshi-trades-on-mention-markets-413058)
- Kalshi reportedly said it had more than a dozen active insider investigations out of about 200 cases reviewed. House Oversight Chair James Comer opened a probe in May 2026 into Kalshi's and Polymarket's insider-trading controls — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/08/09/kalshi-insider-trump-truth-social-prediction-markets-polymarket/?rand=8593); [Willkie summary via search; site not fetchable](https://www.willkie.com/publications/2026/03/cftc-issues-prediction-markets-enforcement-advisory-agency-highlights-disciplinary-cases)
- Federal bills (2026): one tracker counted 14 prediction-market bills (11 distinct proposals). They include Rep. - Resolution-process manipulation: in March 2025 a UMA token "whale" reportedly used about 5M UMA (about 25% of votes) across three wallets to resolve a roughly $7M Polymarket market ("Will Ukraine agree to Trump mineral deal before April?") as YES although no deal had been signed. ### Inferences
- User-created markets on small or obscure events (a person's own actions, a local official, a YouTuber's video) maximize insider and "self-fulfilling" risk. On such markets one participant can determine the outcome, which is both manipulation and, for crimes or harms, an incentive to commit them.
- Design choices that reduce risk: prohibit trading by subjects and insiders in the terms; collect enough KYC to link accounts; run surveillance with documented review; give the platform contractual rights to void trades and claw back winnings; keep an internal-discipline log. Kalshi's program is the template the CFTC credits. Insider trading is not formally "athlete betting" here, but state sports-integrity laws and league rules bar athletes and staff from betting on their sports. [inference; state integrity statutes not researched this session]
- Founder-specific: operators and employees must not trade their own markets. Founder trading on resolution information would be the plainest fraud scenario.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the CFTC advisory text itself (willkie.com and cftc.gov were not fetched).
- No federal insider-trading-on-prediction-markets bill had been enacted as of the sources (mid-2026); I could not confirm status through November 2026.
- State laws specific to prediction-market insider trading were not found.

## 5. Market resolution disputes

### Takeaway
When the operator resolves markets, every ambiguous resolution is a potential breach-of-contract or consumer-protection claim against the operator. Section 230 does not apply to it, and the Khamenei class action shows that even written-but-buried rules invite suits. Polymarket's delegation to the UMA oracle produced repeated disputes (the Ukraine minerals deal in March 2025; the Zelensky suit in July 2025) and reputational damage but, as far as I found, no lawsuit, likely because of its offshore structure and terms. Best practice: precise written criteria fixed before trading, named resolution sources, a death or cancellation carve-out shown at the point of trade, an appeal process, void-and-refund rules for ambiguity, and arbitration with a class-action waiver.

### Cited Findings
- Zelensky "suit" market (June–July 2025): it first resolved YES on July 1, and the UMA dispute process reversed it to NO amid manipulation claims. Reports say fewer than ten UMA holders supplied nearly all of the roughly 18M UMA staked, with three addresses contributing more than 70%. ### Inferences
- In a P2P escrow model the operator is effectively an escrow agent and adjudicator. Wrong resolutions can expose it to breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith, negligence or conversion of escrowed funds, and state UDAP claims. Clear terms reduce this exposure; discretionary "we decide" clauses are vulnerable to unconscionability or good-faith challenges.
- Arbitration and class waivers are generally enforceable under the FAA (AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011) [background]). Their limits: mass-arbitration fee exposure; they do not bind state AGs (as in the NY AG v. Kalshi suit); and if the underlying contracts are illegal gambling, plaintiffs argue the whole agreement is void. Gambling loss-recovery statutes let losers sue to recover losses regardless of the terms (see Section 6).

## 6. Consumer protection, advertising, age and problem gambling, class actions

### Takeaway
The most active private litigation front in 2025–2026 is consumer class actions. They plead that prediction markets are unlicensed gambling and use state consumer-protection and gambling-loss-recovery statutes to seek refunds of losses. More than a dozen have been filed against Kalshi, Polymarket, Robinhood and DraftKings, and the NY AG sued Kalshi for $36B. A small P2P operator would face the same theories with less ability to fund a defense. Marketing such as "not gambling", "risk-free" or "you're just trading" is itself a deception allegation.

### Cited Findings
- First nationwide class action against Kalshi: S.D.N.Y., late November 2025. It alleges unlicensed sports betting disguised as a prediction market and that Kalshi-affiliated market makers (with partners such as Susquehanna) took the other side of consumer bets — [Covers](https://www.covers.com/industry/kalshi-faces-class-action-over-alleged-unlicensed-sports-betting-nov-28-2025); [complaint, CourtListener](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.656144/gov.uscourts.nysd.656144.1.0.pdf)
- Follow-on suits were filed in Illinois (January 2026), Alabama, Oregon (invoking the gambling-loss recovery statute and the Unlawful Trade Practices Act), Georgia and Massachusetts. The Massachusetts suit (Suffolk Superior, April 22, 2026) was brought by a self-excluded gambler. Reynolds v. Kalshi alleges deceptive claims that Kalshi is not a gambling platform — [Casino Beats](https://casinobeats.com/2026/01/09/illinois-class-action-alleges-kalshi-operates-illegal-gambling-platform/); [CDC Gaming (self-excluded)](https://cdcgaming.com/brief/self-excluded-massachusetts-man-sues-kalshi-seeks-class-action/); [Reynolds complaint](https://truthinadvertising.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Reynolds-v-Kalshi-complaint.pdf); [CDC Gaming (deceptive claims)](https://cdcgaming.com/brief/kalshi-faces-class-action-lawsuit-over-deceptive-claims-denying-gambling-platform-status/)

- A national class action over illegal sports betting was reported in September 2026 — [Front Office Sports](https://frontofficesports.com/kalshi-hit-with-nationwide-class-action-over-illegal-sports-betting/)
- NY AG v. Kalshi (July 31, 2026): restitution, treble damages, $100K per solicitation, $36B claimed. NY Gaming Commission had issued a cease-and-desist in October 2025; Kalshi's preliminary-injunction bid against NY was denied in July 2026 and an appeals court refused to stay enforcement — [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/410247/new-york-sues-kalshi); [FOW](https://www.fow.com/insights/new-york-sues-kalshi-for-operating-unlicensed-gambling-business)
- FTC Act Section 5 (15 U.S.C. 45) bars unfair or deceptive acts or practices. State UDAP statutes mirror it and supply private rights of action — [15 U.S.C. 45, LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/45)

### Inferences
- Gambling-loss-recovery statutes (many states, e.g., Oregon's cited above) are a strong private weapon: losers can sue for losses, sometimes treble damages, and sometimes third parties (family members) can sue too. Arbitration clauses are contested in that setting.
- Design choices that reduce risk: 21+ age gating where sports betting is 21+ (18+ is the CFTC-DCM norm but is a target in state suits); robust age and identity verification; self-exclusion honoring (the Massachusetts plaintiff was self-excluded elsewhere); deposit and loss limits; responsible-gambling resources; no "risk-free", "free bet" or "not gambling" marketing; and fee disclosure (the 10% of profit must be clearly shown at trade). Design choices that increase risk: affiliate or influencer marketing to young users, bonus credits, and sports markets.

## 7. Data, privacy and AML-related KYC data (high level)

### Takeaway
A real-money platform with KYC will hold government IDs, financial data and possibly SSNs. That brings it under state privacy and breach-notification laws, possibly the CCPA/CPRA (thresholds: more than $25M revenue, 100K consumers, or 50% revenue from selling data [background]), GLBA if it is a financial institution, and BSA record-keeping if it is a money-services business (MSB). Not collecting KYC to avoid these duties is the BitMEX fact pattern that produced personal criminal pleas.

### Cited Findings
- BitMEX founders' guilty pleas were specifically for failing to implement AML and KYC programs — [CoinMarketCap Academy](https://coinmarketcap.com/academy/article/trump-pardons-3-bitmex-co-founders-following-guilty-pleas-in-money-laundering-case)
- CCPA statute text — [Cal. Civ. Code 1798.140, CA Legislative Info](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1798.140)

### Inferences
- A KYC data breach creates both regulatory exposure and class-action exposure (CCPA private right of action for breaches [background]). Data minimization and vendor-hosted KYC reduce it.

## 8. Insurance (D&O / E&O)

### Takeaway
D&O and E&O cover for gambling-adjacent or crypto startups is available but narrow. Policies commonly carry regulatory, crypto or illegal-acts exclusions, and the "fraud/criminal conduct" and "fines/penalties" exclusions plus uninsurability-of-penalties rules mean insurance will not cover a founder's criminal exposure. At best it covers defense costs until a final adjudication. The D&O Diary flagged prediction markets as an emerging D&O risk in March 2026.

### Cited Findings
- D&O policies covering crypto often exclude regulatory claims, digital-asset theft and token offerings; some carry absolute crypto exclusions. A Relm executive said a regulatory exclusion "erodes the value of the D&O policy almost entirely" — [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/in-house-counsel/crypto-risks-prompt-uptick-in-insurance-exclusions)
- The D&O Diary (March 2026), "Prediction Markets and Emerging D&O Risk", says regulators examining market integrity, insider trading and consumer issues create new D&O exposures (e.g., Gemini's pivot to prediction markets) — [D&O Diary](https://www.dandodiary.com/2026/03/articles/securities-litigation/prediction-markets-and-emerging-do-risk/) (summary via search; full text not fetchable)
- Crypto D&O capacity has risen on regulatory tailwinds — [Business Insurance](https://www.businessinsurance.com/crypto-coverage-rises-on-regulatory-tailwind/)

### Inferences
- An unlicensed operator may struggle to bind coverage at all, since application misrepresentations about licensing can void the policy. Standard D&O "conduct" exclusions and the public-policy bar on insuring criminal fines and disgorgement mean the founder bears criminal and restitution exposure personally. Defense-cost advancement is the main realistic benefit.

## Summary: design choices and their direction of risk (for writer synthesis; inferences drawn from the above)
- Increases risk: permissionless market creation; death, war, crime or private-person markets; no KYC or geo-blocking; the operator resolves at its discretion; a rake on winnings (a "vig"); the operator or affiliates trading; commingled escrow; "not gambling" or "risk-free" marketing; 18+ sports markets; serving NY, MA, IL, OR and other aggressive states.
- Reduces (does not eliminate) risk: pre-approval of markets with a prohibited-topics list; written resolution criteria and sources fixed before trading, with a disclosed death or void carve-out; an appeal process and void-and-refund rules; insider and subject trading bans with surveillance; full KYC/AML; segregated third-party escrow; geofencing; 21+ age gating with responsible-gambling tools; arbitration and class waiver; entity formation with formalities. The only route that substantially removes the gambling and commodities core risk is operating as or through a CFTC-registered DCM (covered by other researchers), and even that did not stop state AG and class actions against Kalshi.
